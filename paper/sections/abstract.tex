\begin{abstract}
On open autoresearch leaderboards, many people point their own AI agents at one shared problem and build on each other's public submissions.
A popular explanation for why this works is cross-model pollination: when someone using one company's model picks up work produced with a different company's model, the result is more likely to be an improvement.
I tested this on the public record of \StudyK{} Yukon leaderboards, including ECDSA.Fail, covering \AnalysisRowsTotal{} scored submissions.
For each submission I recorded which company's model the participant declared and which earlier submission it built on.
I fixed the whole analysis in advance, before the outcomes were joined to the data.
The answer is no measurable effect.
Raw promotion rates were \RawCrossRate{} when building on another company's model and \RawSameRate{} when building on the same company's.
Once each participant is compared with themselves at the same stage of a leaderboard, the chances are essentially equal.
The odds are about \PlainPointPct{} higher, and the data rule out anything beyond roughly \PlainLowPct{} lower or \PlainHighPct{} higher.
That clears the pre-registered bar for calling the effect negligible on average, though a stricter bar set at first would have left it undecided.
ECDSA.Fail alone has too few submissions to decide either way.
The 843-qubit circuit quoted in the post that prompted this study did come from a cross-model step, which shows that such steps happen, not that they help on average.
Code, data and the timestamped pre-registration are public.
\end{abstract}
