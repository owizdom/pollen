\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

The short version: building on another company's model neither helps nor hurts, on average.
The rest of this section gives the numbers behind that sentence.
Every number is generated from \file{results/*.json}, either by the locked analysis code (\file{analysis/numbers.py}) or, for descriptive extras such as raw rates and the prediction interval, by \file{tools/paper_numbers.py}, which sits outside the lock.

\subsection{Who builds on whom}
\label{sec:sample}

The \StudyK{} leaderboards hold \AnalysisRowsTotal{} scored submissions.
The main analysis keeps only submissions that built on one earlier submission by a different participant, with a known company on both sides, inside the period where parents can be traced fairly (Section~\ref{sec:window}).
Five reasons remove the rest, counted once each in a fixed order:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \ExclOwnLineage{} built on the participant's own earlier work (these are used for H2);
  \item \ExclMultiParent{} declared more than one parent;
  \item \ExclNovel{} were new enough code to have no parent, or had no candidate parent;
  \item \ExclUnknownFamily{} had an unknown or mixed company on one side;
  \item \ExclOutsideWindow{} came before the traceable period.
\end{itemize}
That leaves \PrimaryRowsTotal{} submissions.
Participants whose submissions all succeeded or all failed tell the model nothing about the difference, so the fits use the remaining \FitRowsTotal{}.

Most parents were found from the code itself: \TreeShare{} by comparing code trees, \TextShare{} from a base named in the note, and \DeclaredShare{} from Yukon's structured attribution record.

Building on another company's model is the normal case on these leaderboards.
Among all submissions with one parent and known companies, \CellOtherSwitch{} built on another participant's work made with a different company's model, and \CellOtherSame{} on another participant's work made with the same company's.
Within a participant's own line of work, switching company was rare: \CellOwnSwitch{} switches against \CellOwnSame{} that kept the same company.
Figure~\ref{fig:flow} shows the flows between companies; Anthropic and OpenAI dominate both sides, and the largest cross-company flows run between them.

\subsection{The main result}
\label{sec:primary}

Raw promotion rates point slightly against cross-company building.
Of the \RawCrossRows{} submissions that built on another company's model, \RawCrossPromoted{} were promoted (\RawCrossRate{}).
Of the \RawSameRows{} that built on the same company's model, \RawSamePromoted{} were promoted (\RawSameRate{}).
These raw rates mix together who submitted, when, and from which starting point, so they are not the test.

The pre-registered test compares each participant with themselves: their cross-company attempts against their own same-company attempts, at the same stage of the leaderboard, with the same starting-point information (Section~\ref{sec:model}).
Done separately on each leaderboard and then combined, it gives an odds ratio of \PooledOR{}.
In words, the odds of promotion are about \PlainPointPct{} higher when building on another company's model, which is no real difference.
The 90\% interval runs from \PooledORNinetyLow{} to \PooledORNinetyHigh{}: the data rule out a disadvantage bigger than about \PlainLowPct{} or an advantage bigger than about \PlainHighPct{}.
The 95\% interval is \PooledORLow{} to \PooledORHigh{}.

The plan fixed in advance that an effect counts as negligible if the 90\% interval sits entirely inside 0.67 to 1.50.
It does, so the pre-registered verdict is \emph{\PooledVerdict{}}.
Figure~\ref{fig:forest} shows each leaderboard and the combined estimate.

That bar was not the first one.
The plan began with a stricter bar of 0.80 to 1.25, and under it the result would be undecided, because the upper end, \PooledORNinetyHighFine{}, sits just above 1.25.
Before any outcome was joined to the data, a simulation using only the structure of the data showed that the stricter bar would reach a verdict only \PowerEquivalence{} of the time even if there were truly no effect, against \PowerWidened{} for the wider bar.
A rule written on 17 October, before the data were frozen, said to switch to the wider bar in that case, and the switch is recorded in the locked plan (Section~\ref{sec:equivalence}).

Two further points sharpen what the verdict covers.
First, it is about the average across leaderboards.
Leaderboards differ a little from each other (between-leaderboard variance $\tau^2 = \PooledTauSquared{}$), and a 90\% prediction interval for the effect on a new leaderboard runs from \PredLow{} to \PredHigh{}.
So the data are consistent with no effect on average while leaving room for modest effects, either way, on a single leaderboard; this interval was not part of the plan.
Second, every leaderboard was fitted with Firth's method, a standard correction for small or lopsided groups, instead of the planned conditional model.
On eight leaderboards the reason was the one the plan names: some rarely used companies had no promoted submissions at all, or only promoted ones, which breaks the planned model.
On \bench{davidtai/qwen38-125b-a6b-cuda-v1} and \bench{eigenlabs/eip8200-challenges/modexp} the switch came from a safety rule in the locked code, not stated in the plan's text, that rejects any fit with a coefficient or standard error above 15; the time-trend terms crossed it there.
The planned model never failed on the cross-company comparison itself.
The estimates follow the locked code, and \file{docs/DEVIATIONS.md} records the mismatch.

\subsection{ECDSA.Fail on its own}
\label{sec:ecdsa}

On ECDSA.Fail alone the odds ratio is \BenchJOR{}, from \BenchJFitRows{} submissions and \BenchJStrataKept{} informative participants.
The 95\% interval runs from \BenchJORLow{} to \BenchJORHigh{} and the 90\% interval from \EcdsaNinetyLow{} to \EcdsaNinetyHigh{}.
The best guess points against cross-company building, but the interval is wide enough to include anything from a large disadvantage to a small advantage, so the verdict is \emph{\CaseStudyVerdict{}}.
This was expected: the plan noted before the lock that ECDSA.Fail alone is too small to settle the question (Section~\ref{sec:equivalence}), which is why the main test combines all ten leaderboards.
As one of the secondary tests, its $p$-value is \SecHOneJP{}, or \SecHOneJPHolm{} after correcting for the number of tests.

\subsection{Secondary questions}
\label{sec:secondary-results}

The plan also asked four narrower questions (Section~\ref{sec:secondary}); Table~\ref{tab:secondary} in Appendix~\ref{app:secondary} lists every test.
A test only counts when the smaller group has at least 20 successes, so that it is not decided by a handful of events.
\HolmFamilySize{} tests passed that bar: ECDSA.Fail's main test, the size-of-gain test (H4) on each of the \StudyK{} leaderboards, and the Pareto test (H5) on ECDSA.Fail.
After correcting for running that many tests, none shows an effect; the smallest corrected $p$-value is \HolmMinP{}.
Switching company within your own line of work (H2) was too rare, and the large-jump and plateau-break questions (H3a, H3b) had too few events, to be tested on any leaderboard, so they are reported only as description.
Large jumps by other participants number between 1 and 10 per leaderboard in the smaller group, and plateau breaks at most one.

One leaderboard, \ExcludesOneBench{}, has a 95\% interval that excludes 1 (uncorrected $p = \ExcludesOneP{}$).
The per-leaderboard estimates are pieces of the combined estimate, not separate tests, and with ten leaderboards about one interval in twenty, so half of one, is expected to miss 1 by chance.

\subsection{Exploratory analyses}
\label{sec:exploratory}

These were not part of the main plan and carry no correction.
E1 compares the \EOneMultiRows{} submissions that mixed companies or declared several parents with the \EOneSingleRows{} that did not; most leaderboards have too few mixed submissions for a stable estimate, and the results are in \file{results/exploratory.json}.
E2 asks whether the effect changes over a leaderboard's life by splitting each one into thirds by time.
The combined odds ratios are \ExploreEThirdOneOR{}, \ExploreEThirdTwoOR{} and \ExploreEThirdThreeOR{} for the first, middle and last third, all close to 1.
